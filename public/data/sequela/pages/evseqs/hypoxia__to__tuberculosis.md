# Hypoxia → Tuberculosis

**Hypothesis in plain terms**  
The hypothesis of a causal link between ancestral exposure to high altitude hypoxia and high susceptibility to tuberculosis in descendants is due to Corbett et al. (2022). Their reasoning is as follows. Among Nepali migrants to Australia, unusually high rates of tuberculosis (TB) have been observed—even though people are screened before entry and local transmission explains only a small fraction of cases. These patterns suggest that standard proximate explanations (pre-existing disease, transmission after arrival, or background prevalence) are not sufficient. A plausible additional factor is ancestry at high altitude: genetic adaptations to hypoxia (notably variants in the HIF pathway such as *EGLN1/EPAS1*) may dampen hypoxia-driven inflammatory responses, which becomes disadvantageous after descent to sea level, increasing the chance that latent TB reactivates.

**Why this seems to fit more than one population**  
Reports summarized by Corbett *et al.* show similarly high TB burdens in other groups with high-altitude ancestry after moving to lowland settings—for example, Tibetans (persistently high TB in Dharamshala; elevated latent and active TB among Tibetan refugees in the USA) and migrants from the Andean and Ethiopian highlands (e.g., Peruvian and Bolivian migrants with excess or clustered TB). These parallels indicate the pattern is not unique to Nepalis and may generalize to other high-altitude populations.

**What the data say in NSW (Australia)**
- Nepali migrants had TB incidence comparable to rates in Nepal itself and substantially higher than other migrant groups, with the excess concentrated in the first ~5 years after arrival. Local genomic clustering was low. 
- The pattern is consistent with a higher risk of reactivation at sea level among people with high-altitude adaptations; authors estimate that such adaptations could account for a meaningful share of excess TB cases.
