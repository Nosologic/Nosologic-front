# Malaria → Sickle cell disease (evolutionary sequela)

**Source:** [malaria](/diseases-md/malaria)  
**Target:** [sickle cell disease](/diseases-md/sickle-cell-disease)

---

## Summary
The sickle cell allele (HbS) is a single amino-acid substitution in **HBB** that causes sickle cell disease in homozygotes, but in heterozygotes (AS) it confers substantial protection against severe *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria. Classic cohort and case–control studies—beginning with Allison in the 1950s—showed reduced parasite densities and markedly lower risk of severe outcomes among AS individuals, especially in young children before acquired immunity develops. The selective advantage to heterozygotes has been strong enough that, in regions of high malaria transmission, natural selection raised HbS frequencies despite the cost of homozygous disease. This is a textbook example of **balanced polymorphism** (heterozygote advantage), where malaria is the selective environment and sickle cell disease represents the clinical sequela when two copies of the variant are inherited.

## Geographical evidence
HbS frequency closely mirrors the historical distribution of intense falciparum malaria: common across sub-Saharan Africa and present at appreciable levels in parts of the Mediterranean and South Asia, but rare where malaria was absent. This spatial concordance—established in early mapping and repeatedly confirmed—remains a key line of evidence for malaria-driven selection.

## Additional evidence
- **Epidemiology:** Heterozygotes have substantially reduced odds of severe malaria.
- **Clinical:** AS individuals carry lower parasite burdens and have better outcomes during infection.
- **Mechanistic:** In vitro work supports impaired parasite growth in HbS erythrocytes and enhanced clearance of infected cells.
