# Hypoxia → Tuberculosis

**Source disease/condition:** [hypoxia](/diseases/hypoxia)  
**Target disease:** [tuberculosis](/diseases/tuberculosis)

## Concept (brief)
The hypothesis is that chronic hypoxia at altitude modifies host immunity via HIF-mediated pathways. In populations adapted to altitude (e.g., Tibetan and some Nepali groups), descent to normoxia may blunt hypoxia-driven inflammatory responses that normally help constrain *M. tuberculosis*, increasing the likelihood of reactivation or progression. This frames TB as an **evolutionary sequela** of long-term hypoxic living conditions once people relocate to low altitude.

## Geographical evidence
- Persistently high TB incidence among Nepalese and Tibetan migrants in low-incidence settings; most excess risk is observed in the first years after migration.
- In high-altitude regions within several countries, TB incidence tends to be lower than in lowlands; descent (or residence at low altitude) aligns with higher TB risk.
- Nepal stands out: TB incidence among Nepalese immigrants in parts of Australia remained comparable to in-country rates, with age distribution and timing of cases pointing away from local transmission.

**See cluster:** [Nepal](/clusters/nepal)

## Biological/host evidence (summary)
- High-altitude adaptations frequently involve HIF-pathway genes (e.g., **EPAS1** / HIF-2α; **EGLN1** / PHD2 variants).
- These variants can blunt erythropoietic and inflammatory responses to hypoxia; upon descent, a relatively reduced hypoxia-inflammation tone may lessen host control of latent TB.
- Experimental and clinical observations support hypoxia’s capacity to constrain mycobacteria and to modulate immune responses.

## Caveats
- This is a population-level, evolutionary hypothesis; individual risk reflects multiple drivers (infection history, crowding, nutrition, etc.). Further case-control and mechanistic studies are needed.

## References
- Corbett S, Cho J-G, Ulbricht E, Sintchenko V. **Migration and descent, adaptations to altitude and tuberculosis in Nepalis and Tibetans** (open access). Evolution, Medicine & Public Health, 2022.
